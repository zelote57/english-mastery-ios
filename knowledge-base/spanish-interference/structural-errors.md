# A1: Spanish interference and sentence structure

Spanish and English often express the same idea using different sentence structures. Translating every word separately can produce a sentence that sounds familiar to a Spanish speaker but is not grammatical in standard English. Look at the whole sentence: find its subject, verb, objects, and descriptive words.

English normally requires an explicit subject, even when Spanish leaves it out. Use **I**, **you**, **he**, **she**, **it**, **we**, or **they**, as appropriate. For weather, English commonly uses the subject **it**: “It is raining.” In a statement, the usual English order is subject, verb, and then object: “She reads books.”

English adjectives commonly come before the noun: “a red car.” Spanish often places an adjective after a noun, so transferring that order directly can lead to “a car red.” Also note that in standard English a negative verb usually combines with **anything**, **anyone**, or **anywhere**, not another negative word. For example, say “I don't have anything” or “I have nothing,” rather than combining both negative forms.

Compare:

- ❌ “Is raining.” → ✅ “It is raining.” — Está lloviendo.
- ❌ “A car red.” → ✅ “A red car.” — Un coche rojo.
- ❌ “I don't have nothing.” → ✅ “I don't have anything.” — No tengo nada.
- ❌ “She reads every day books.” → ✅ “She reads books every day.” — Ella lee libros todos los días.

These are general patterns, not a reason to translate every Spanish expression literally. When correcting a sentence, first check whether the English subject is present. Then check the order of the verb, its object, and the adjective. Finally, check whether the negative words fit the intended meaning.
